About

I study how K-pop gets made — the people, the studios, and the industrial conditions that shape it as sound.

I'm a PhD candidate in Ethnomusicology at the University of Pennsylvania, currently in the fieldwork stage of my dissertation. Before starting my PhD, I worked for years as a jazz and pop pianist, composer, arranger, and bandleader — time spent on the other side of the studio glass that still shapes the questions I bring to my research today.

My work sits at the intersection of ethnomusicology and political economy, with a long-standing interest in how the structures of the music industry — credit, copyright, collaboration — leave their imprint on the sound itself.

Research

My dissertation,

Assembling the Sound: Labor, Capital, and Experimental Production of K-pop

asks why K-pop sounds the way it does — hybrid, genre-promiscuous, resistant to a single style — and argues that this hybridity is not simply an aesthetic choice but a structural consequence of the multi-member idol group as a business model. Drawing on ethnographic fieldwork with songwriters, producers, and A&R professionals, I trace how the neoliberal constraints of the industry paradoxically generate conditions for musical experimentation, and how a song's sonic structure can carry the direct imprint of the industrial conditions that produced it.

The project is grounded in social reproduction theory and debates on cognitive and platform capitalism, in conversation with foundational ethnomusicological work on musical labor and collaborative practice.
